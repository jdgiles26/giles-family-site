Prologue

For Joshua, and for everyone who sits at the Giles table.

This is the story of a family that came over the mountains from Virginia, put down roots in the creek bottoms and ridges of south-central Kentucky, and never really let go of them — even when the work, the war and the factories called them north. It is told from the records they left behind: census pages written in iron-gall ink, a hometown newspaper that noticed every wedding, soldier and funeral, and one creased Army discharge that William’s daughter Alice kept safe for decades and passed down to her nephew Joshua.

Chapter I · Generation 1

Over the Mountains

Elijah Giles · born c. 1794, Virginia

Every family story needs a first footstep, and ours belongs to . He was born in Virginia around 1794, in the years when Kentucky was brand-new and the Wilderness Road was the great highway west for Virginia families.

By 1840 Elijah was heading a household of nine in Casey County — two of the boys in their late teens. Ten years later the census-taker found him in Rockcastle County, a 56-year-old labourer with his wife Susan and four children still at home. And on the very next line of the page, in the house next door, lived a young farmer named James.

In 1860, at 66, Elijah was still on Rockcastle ground, his son Samuel beside him.

Chapter II · Generation 2

Three Houses in a Row

James W. Giles (1824–1913) & Abigail “Abby” C.

On 29 August 1850 an assistant marshal named M. H. Rielly rode the northern district of Rockcastle County with his census book. At dwelling 158 he wrote down the Prindle family. At 159: James Giles, 26, farmer, his wife Abagail, 21, born in Virginia, and baby John, one year old. At 160: Elijah himself, 56. Abby’s people on one side, James’s father on the other.

Within twenty years James and Abby had moved the family west to the Neatsville district of Adair County — the place the Giles name would belong to for the next century. In 1870 there were ten under their roof, five boys listed as “working on farm.” James was still farming at 58 in 1880, and he lived to 88. He died on 19 June 1913 and rests in the Tabernacle Cemetery at Neatsville.

Chapter III · Generation 3

Two Branches at Pellyton

William Preston Giles (b. 1851) · James Washington Giles (1853–1938)

Look closely at the 1870 page and two names sit one above the other: William P., 19 and Washington, 17, both “working on farm.” They were and , and from them grew two branches of the Adair County Giles.

They grew up in the years of the Civil War and grew old as neighbors. By 1880 Washington had a home of his own at Casey Creek (the census-taker wrote his initials as “K. W.”). By 1900 the two households stood side by side in the Pellyton district: Preston and Susan with Luther, Arthur Owen and Melcena; Washington and Mary with Jared and a nine-year-old called Thomas F. — Frank — who would one day be the father of Henry Earl Giles.

Preston’s son Luther and Washington’s son Frank grew up one fence apart.

Chapter IV · Generation 4

Married in Tennessee

Luther Aaron Giles (1882–1959) & Nora V. Allen (c. 1890–1975)
“Mr. Luther Giles and Miss Tinna Allen eloped to Tennessee a few days ago, and got married.”— Knifley news, Adair County News, 19 September 1906

That one line is all the paper gave it, and it is plenty. , Preston’s eldest son, was in his twenties. — “Tiny” to the people who loved her — was sixteen. They crossed into Tennessee to be married and came home to build a life together that lasted more than fifty years.

They raised seven sons and two daughters on Adair County farmland. In 1950 the census found Luther, 69, and Nora, 60, on the farm at Blue Hollow off Ky 76, with Walter, Russell and Elvin still at home — and William, Melzena, Herman and Zackie all within the same district. When Luther died at his home in the Neatsville community on 22 January 1959, the Adair County News listed his children from Adair County to Edinburg and Columbus, Indiana, to Wadsworth, Ohio.

Chapter V · Generation 5

Rifleman

William Cecil Giles · 19 April 1924, Knifley – 16 March 2001

was nineteen, a young farmer from Knifley, when the Army called him in October 1943. By the summer of 1944 he was in France, a rifleman with Company E, 9th Infantry. On 3 August 1944 he was hit — severe leg wounds — and came home with a Purple Heart.

He married in the spring of 1945, came home to Adair County on 1 June. Like many Kentucky veterans he went where the work was: the 1950 census notes he had been living in Wadsworth, Ohio, in 1949; by 1959 the family lived at Edinburg, Indiana, and their children went to school at Indian Creek. He retired from Cosco Industries and spent his last years at Edmonton, Kentucky, a member of the Baptist faith. He rests at Heavenly Hill Cemetery in Columbia.

Chapter VI · William & Bertha

A Kentucky Home in Indiana

William Cecil Giles & Bertha (Burton) Giles · married spring 1945

was the daughter of John R. Burton, whose family lived in the Purdy community of Adair County. While William was overseas, Bertha was living up in Indiana: the Purdy column of the Adair County News placed “Miss Bertha Burton” at Edinburg, Indiana, in December 1944. By April 1945 the same column called her “Mrs. Bertha Giles,” home on a visit to her parents.

In April 1950 the young couple were back in the Casey Creek district, keeping house off Ky 206, in the same census district as Luther and Nora’s farm at Blue Hollow. William was 25 and Bertha was 24, with Evelyn, two, and baby Bernadean. The census-taker noted that William had spent part of 1949 working in Wadsworth, Ohio.

Within a few years the family had made Johnson County, Indiana, their home. Cecil Jr. was born in Franklin in 1954 and Hershel in 1959. When Luther died that year, the paper listed “William Giles, Edinburg, Ind.,” and in 1975 Nora’s obituary placed him at Trafalgar. The children went to Indian Creek High School: Cecil Jr. graduated in 1973 and Hershel in 1977. William and Bertha raised eleven children in all.

Bertha died before William. In later years he returned to south-central Kentucky, retired from Cosco Industries and made his home at Edmonton with his second wife, . When he died in 2001 he left ten living children, twenty grandchildren and five great-grandchildren.

1954 – 2019

The fourth of eleven · the athlete and builder

Born in Franklin on 16 December 1954, Cecil was his father’s namesake and one of the finest athletes Indian Creek High School ever produced. He pitched and played infield for sectional champions in 1971 and 1973, played in the 1971 Johnson County Tournament, and was named MVP and All-Conference in both baseball and basketball. He was the first Indian Creek athlete to earn a college scholarship in two sports.

He took a degree in education at Auburn University and spent three years teaching and coaching in the Cobb County schools of Marietta, Georgia. Then, like his brother Hershel, he turned to building. For more than thirty years he owned Cecil Giles Custom Homes in Georgia and Indiana, and he retired from Carpenters Union Local 301 in 2018. Cecil died at 64 on 15 August 2019, after two years of fighting glioblastoma. His funeral was held in the Indian Creek gymnasium, where he had starred as a student.

Chapter VI · Hershel

The Builder

Hershel Dewayne Giles · 3 July 1959 – 14 November 2022

was born in Franklin, Indiana, on 3 July 1959, one of William and Bertha’s eleven children and part of the generation that grew up north of the Ohio River. He graduated from Indian Creek High School in 1977 and studied Building Trades at Ivy Tech.

He made his living with a framing hammer. Self-employed, Hershel framed houses all over Johnson, Morgan and Brown counties. He lived for years in Nineveh and later in Martinsville.

Off the job he cruised in his ’72 Chevy Nova, went boating and skiing, hunted mushrooms in the spring, tended a vegetable garden and worked wood. He followed NASCAR, pulling for Dale Earnhardt Sr. and Chase Elliott, and he followed IU men’s basketball. He loved caring for his German shepherd, Pal. Above all he loved his family, especially his children, Shelby and Joshua, and he was the man people called when they needed a hand.

Hershel died at his home in Martinsville on 14 November 2022, at 63. After a funeral at Meredith-Clark Funeral Home on 19 November, he was buried in East Hill Cemetery in Morgantown.

A branch worth climbing

The Novelist’s Husband

Remember Washington’s boy Frank, who grew up next door to Luther? His son (1916–1986) met an Arkansas-born writer named Janice Holt on a two-day bus trip in 1943. He went to war as an Army sergeant; she wrote to him; they married on 11 October 1945.

In 1949 they moved to the Giles Ridge farm in Adair County, and turned the ridges and hollows of Giles country into literature: The Enduring Hills, the warm and funny Giles Ridge memoir 40 Acres and No Mule, The Kentuckians, Hannah Fowler, The Believers. Henry published a novel of his own, Harbin’s Ridge. When they built their log house at Spout Springs, Henry’s father Frank rived the shingles for its roof by hand: ten thousand of them, according to the house’s National Register nomination.

Their wartime letters and Henry’s war diary survive among Janice Holt Giles’s papers at Western Kentucky University.
