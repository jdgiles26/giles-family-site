A family legend

The Daughter of Dragging Canoe

A story told at the Giles table

Every old family has a story that isn’t written down anywhere — the one that gets told on the porch after supper. In the Giles family it goes like this: we are kin to the daughter of Chief Dragging Canoe. It has been carried down among William Cecil Giles’s children, and it is shared here the way the family tells it, as a treasured inheritance.

The chief

Dragging Canoe — in Cherokee, Tsiyu Gansini — was one of the great figures of the American frontier. He was the son of Attakullakulla, the Overhill Cherokee leader the English called “Little Carpenter.”

In March 1775, at Sycamore Shoals on the Watauga River, Richard Henderson’s Transylvania Company bargained with Cherokee leaders for an enormous tract of land — much of what would become Kentucky. Dragging Canoe would not agree to it. Tradition says he warned the buyers that the land would become a dark and bloody ground.

He held to his refusal. From about 1777 he led the Chickamauga, the Lower Cherokee, in a long struggle to defend their homeland, known as the Chickamauga Wars. He died at Running Water Town on 1 March 1792, after a diplomatic journey to rally other nations to the cause.

The daughter

Written history kept far more about Dragging Canoe’s battles than about his household. The historian E. Raymond Evans, writing in the Journal of Cherokee Studies, recorded a son, known as Young or Little Dragging Canoe. Much of Cherokee family history was kept the way the Giles family has kept this story: by word of mouth, one generation to the next.

The Giles family has carried the memory of the chief’s daughter for generations, and it is honored here as part of who the family is. Elijah Giles came into Kentucky within a generation of Dragging Canoe’s war, and his descendants made their homes in those same hills. A story kept this long is a gift to every generation that hears it.
